# Module 8: Retention and Revenue Expansion

## What to analyze

- Onboarding and activation: does the customer reach a genuine "aha" moment, and how quickly
- Customer experience post-purchase
- Retention patterns and churn risk signals
- Repeat purchase behavior (where applicable)
- Referral behavior
- Upsell and cross-sell opportunities, where they genuinely add value rather than just extracting more revenue
- Lifecycle communication (email/in-app sequences across the customer's life with the product)
- Customer lifetime value, only where there's enough data to estimate it meaningfully

## Cross-sell hypotheses

Treat cross-sell potential as a HYPOTHESIS until customer behavior validates it. Buying a digital product does not, by itself, establish willingness to pay for a SaaS product or prove that buyers are not already converting. Before ranking cross-sell as a leading opportunity, check audience overlap, customer intent, existing cross-sell activity, conversion potential, and relevant economics. Minimum validation is a defined buyer cohort with exposure and conversion events tracked over a stated period, followed by a small, measurable cross-sell test if the baseline is unknown. Distinguish expressed interest, trial starts, and paid conversion.

## Adapt to business model

Retention means something different depending on the model:

- **Subscription SaaS**: activation speed, feature adoption depth, usage frequency, renewal and expansion
- **Ecommerce**: repeat purchase rate, time between purchases, average order value growth
- **Services/agency**: project outcomes, relationship depth, contract renewal or expansion
- **Marketplace**: both sides' retention (supply and demand), liquidity over time
- **One-time purchase products**: referral and reviews matter more than repeat purchase

Don't apply subscription-style retention thinking (MRR, churn rate) to a business model where it doesn't fit.

## Activation before retention

If activation is weak (new customers never reach the point where the product's value is obvious), retention tactics (win-back emails, loyalty programs) are a lower-leverage fix than repairing onboarding. Check activation first; it's frequently the actual bottleneck hiding behind what looks like a retention problem.

## Don't invent LTV

Only state observed LTV from contribution actually observed for a defined customer cohort and observation window. A modelled LTV is a separate estimate: state the formula, inputs, time horizon, and assumptions, and do not present it as observed cohort retention.

An implied average lifetime calculated as `1 / churn rate` is only a model estimate under a stable, constant churn hazard and consistent cohort/period definitions. State those assumptions and the unit of time. It is not an observed retention curve, does not establish how long each customer stays, and must not substitute for cohort retention data. If cohort data is insufficient, do not declare retention the primary business problem solely from churn or implied lifetime.

## Output for this module

When requested, or when Module 1 diagnosis points to retention as the real bottleneck (common for subscription businesses with visible churn or ecommerce with low repeat rate). Prioritize the first lifecycle moment most likely to be leaking value, rather than a full lifecycle map by default.
