# Module 2: Market and Competitor Intelligence

## What to analyze

- Market structure (fragmented vs. consolidated, growing vs. shrinking, how customers currently solve the problem)
- Direct competitors (same solution, same customer)
- Indirect competitors and substitutes (different solution, same underlying job, including "doing nothing" or a manual/spreadsheet workaround)
- Competitive positioning: where competitors sit, what they claim, what they're known for
- Differentiation: what's genuinely different about this business, not just different wording for the same thing
- Underserved segments or unmet needs visible in the market
- Competitive advantages this business actually has (not aspirational ones)
- Market threats: new entrants, platform risk, commoditization pressure

## Verified research vs. assumption

This is the module where it's most tempting to fabricate. Don't.

- If browsing or another live research tool is available and the request warrants it, use current, relevant sources, prefer reliable primary sources, check publication dates, and cite external claims plainly.
- If live research is unavailable, state that limitation and work only from accessible Business Context and supporting documents. Treat user claims as unverified, not as current external facts.
- Never invent competitor pricing, feature lists, market size figures, or market share numbers. If you don't know, say "unknown" or "would need research to confirm" rather than producing a plausible-sounding number.
- A user-named competitor with no further detail should be treated as a known entity whose specifics are unverified, not as an opportunity to generate plausible-sounding facts about them.

## Differentiation test

A claimed differentiator only counts if it would actually change a customer's decision. Run every proposed differentiator through this: "if a competitor said the exact same sentence, would a customer notice or care?" Generic claims ("great customer service," "easy to use," "affordable") usually fail this test and should be flagged as weak rather than presented as real positioning.

## Market gaps

A real market gap is a combination of: a customer need that's clearly underserved, a segment competitors are ignoring or serving poorly, and a reason this business specifically is positioned to serve it. All three need to be present. "Nobody else does X" alone isn't a gap if nobody wants X.

## Output for this module

Feeds into the Business Diagnosis and the Opportunity Radar. Usually doesn't need its own section in the final brief unless the user specifically asked for competitive analysis, in which case present: 2-3 real competitors/substitutes, where this business is differentiated (or isn't), and the clearest gap or threat.
