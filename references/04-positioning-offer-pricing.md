# Module 4: Positioning, Offer, and Pricing

## What to develop

- Market positioning: what category the business competes in, in the customer's mind
- Category strategy: compete in an existing category, redefine it, or create a new one
- Differentiation that passes the test in Module 2 (would a customer notice if a competitor said the same thing?)
- Core value proposition, stated in one or two sentences a customer would actually say back
- Messaging hierarchy: the one main claim, then 2-3 supporting proof points, not a dozen co-equal features
- Offer architecture: what's actually being sold, bundled, tiered
- Pricing hypotheses (only where there's enough context to reason about willingness to pay; never invent a number from nothing)
- Packaging and bundling logic
- Risk reversal (guarantees, trials, low-commitment entry points) where it addresses a real objection from Module 3
- Perceived value relative to price, not just price in isolation
- Competitive offer comparison where relevant

## Positioning test

Good positioning answers, specifically: for whom, for what problem, instead of what alternative, and why this is better. If you can't fill in all four from available context, the positioning is underdeveloped, and that's worth saying directly rather than papering over with generic language.

## Pricing discipline

Only recommend specific price changes or figures when there's real context to reason from: current pricing, competitor pricing, customer willingness-to-pay signals, or margin data. Otherwise, discuss pricing strategy and structure (value-based vs. cost-plus, tiering logic, anchor pricing) without inventing a dollar figure. It's fine to say "consider testing a price increase" with reasoning; it's not fine to assert "$79 is the right price" without evidence.

## Common positioning failures to check for

- Positioning against a feature rather than an outcome
- Trying to be the best option for everyone (usually means being the clear best option for no one)
- Differentiation that's true but doesn't matter to the customer
- Price that signals the wrong category (too cheap for a premium claim, too expensive without the proof to back it)
- Messaging that leads with the company/product instead of the customer's problem

## Output for this module

Core part of the comprehensive brief when positioning is weak or unclear; this is frequently where the "Biggest Marketing Weakness" in the Opportunity Radar lives, since muddy positioning quietly undermines every other marketing effort.
