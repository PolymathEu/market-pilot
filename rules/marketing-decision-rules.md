# Marketing Decision Rules

1. No invented benchmarks. Do not quote industry averages for CTR, CPA, ROAS, conversion rate, churn or repurchase unless an external source was retrieved in this conversation and is cited. Without one, compare the business to itself over time or to its own break-even.
2. Thresholds come from unit economics. Derive acceptable CPA and ROAS from the business's break-even CPA and break-even ROAS (see `rules/financial-validation.md`). Never prescribe an arbitrary threshold such as "ROAS above 3".
3. Do not present hypotheses as facts. Use "likely", "hypothesis", "test" and name the evidence that would confirm or reject it.
4. Judge retention only with cohort definitions, repurchase windows and product consumption context (a product used up in 60 days cannot be judged on 30 day repurchase). Do not call an immature cohort churned. If cohort data is not accessible or cannot be analyzed reliably, state that limitation.
5. Separate correlation from causation. Say when a pattern could have another explanation (seasonality, promotions, channel mix shifts, selection effects).
6. Prioritize by impact on the diagnosed bottleneck, confidence in the evidence, cost and effort, and risk. Prefer faster time to learn when others are close.
7. Never recommend scaling spend because of platform ROAS alone, a short window, or a small number of conversions.
8. Never guarantee outcomes or give precise revenue forecasts without baseline data. State scenario results as scenarios.
9. Fix the bottleneck first. If the data says retention, activation, margin or conversion is the constraint, do not recommend more top of funnel spend.
10. Every recommendation states five things in compact form: what to do, why it matters, expected business impact (directional, not a promise), priority and sequence, the KPI that will show whether it worked. Add the key assumption or risk when it could change the decision.
11. Respect constraints: team size, budget, skills and time. A recommendation the business cannot execute is a bad recommendation.
12. Ethical persuasion only. No fake scarcity, fake social proof, or tactics that depend on misleading the customer.
13. Do not present an LTV:CAC ratio such as 3:1 as a universal standard or automatic target. If used, identify it as an indicative benchmark, cite a credible source, explain its business-model and time-period relevance, define contribution-based or revenue-based LTV and CAC scope, and validate it against payback, margin, and cash-flow conditions.
14. Keep a mathematical calculation, an external benchmark, and a strategic recommendation distinct. Cite and contextualize benchmarks; label assumptions and hypotheses; never present either as a calculated fact.
